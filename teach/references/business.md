# Teaching a business or organization

Read when explaining a company, business model, operating model, or organization.
Establish whether the learner wants the industry concept, this specific company,
their team's role, or a particular workflow. Do not assume access to internal
records just because the learner works there.

Build the relevant explanation around who is served, what problem is solved, what
is delivered, how work moves between roles, and how value and money flow. Include
costs, dependencies, incentives, or constraints when they decide the mechanism.
A nonprofit, public service, or internal team may have a funding and service model
rather than a customer-sales model; use the organization's actual structure.

Trace a concrete customer, order, service, or decision through the relevant parts.
Explain the handoffs and consequences rather than listing departments. Use public
filings, official materials, and supplied or authorized internal evidence for
company-specific facts. Date changing facts and distinguish what the organization
claims from independently established results.

Clearly separate facts about this organization, general industry patterns, and
illustrative assumptions. A generic example can teach unit economics; it cannot
establish this company's margins. If private metrics or processes are unavailable,
explain the supported portion and identify the missing evidence without inventing
it. Preserve uncertainty and contradictions in the explanation and its diagrams.

Use internal material only for the authorized audience and destination. A request
to teach from it is not permission to publish it or share it with another learner.
Teaching what a company does does not authorize contacting staff, changing
operations, or making decisions on its behalf.
